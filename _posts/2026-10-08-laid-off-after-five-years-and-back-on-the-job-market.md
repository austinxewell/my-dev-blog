Well, I guess it's time to write one of these.

After nearly five years at StarkSys, I was laid off.

It still feels weird to say that.

Five years is a long time. When I started at StarkSys, I was still early in my development career. A lot has changed since then. I became a much more capable developer, worked on projects that pushed me outside of my comfort zone, learned how to work on production applications used by real people, and gradually took on more responsibility as an engineer.

StarkSys ended up being a huge part of my career.

I worked on websites, SaaS applications, internal tools, and a whole lot of frontend code. I spent most of my time working with Vue, Nuxt, TypeScript, REST APIs, and the various pieces that come with actually maintaining production software. Over time, I went from being one developer on a team to owning frontend development across multiple projects.

There were plenty of frustrating days, of course. That's software development.

There were also a lot of projects that I'm genuinely proud of.

One of them was a Complaint Management Portal that I essentially owned on the frontend. I handled the Nuxt application, Pinia state management, TypeScript, Tailwind, GitLab repository, CI/CD pipelines, development environments, code quality tooling, and the authentication flow. The application also had to integrate with an FDA-approved database for food product complaints and meet some pretty serious security requirements.

That kind of work taught me something that tutorials and bootcamps can't really teach you: eventually, somebody is depending on the thing you built to actually work.

That's a different feeling from getting a project to work on your laptop.

## So, what's next?

I'm now back on the job market looking for my next opportunity as a Frontend Engineer or a Frontend-focused Software Engineer.

My strongest experience is still frontend development. Vue and Nuxt are where I'm most comfortable, with TypeScript being a big part of how I build applications. I've also worked with React, Node, REST APIs, SQL and NoSQL databases, Docker, CI/CD, and the other pieces that tend to surround modern web applications.

I'm particularly interested in finding a role where I can continue growing beyond frontend development as well.

I've spent years getting good at building interfaces and frontend architecture. I'd like my next role to give me more opportunities to work on backend systems and AI/LLM technologies too.

And, honestly, the AI part is hard to ignore.

AI has changed the software development landscape pretty dramatically in a relatively short amount of time. It's one of the reasons I'm interested in expanding my skills instead of simply trying to find another job where I do exactly what I've been doing for the last five years.

I've already started building AI into my own projects, including a chatbot on my personal portfolio that can answer questions about my experience and skills. I'm interested in going much further with that.

I'm not under the impression that adding "AI" to my resume magically solves anything. It doesn't.

But I do think it's worth learning how these tools actually work and figuring out where they can make me a better engineer.

## The uncomfortable part

There's another part of this that I'm still figuring out.

I don't have a college degree.

For the last several years, that wasn't really a problem. I got my foot in the door, built real software, accumulated experience, and kept learning. My experience became more important than the fact that I didn't have a bachelor's degree.

Now I'm back in the job market at a very different point in the industry.

Software development is changing because of AI. Companies are cutting teams. Job postings can have hundreds or thousands of applicants. Some positions require degrees before a human ever looks at the application.

And I'm competing with people who have degrees, more years of experience, different technology backgrounds, and sometimes all three.

I'm not going to pretend that doesn't make me nervous.

At the same time, I don't think the answer is to convince myself that the last five years don't count.

They do.

I've built and maintained real production software. I've worked on applications used by real customers and internal teams. I've dealt with deadlines, bugs, changing requirements, deployments, code reviews, legacy code, authentication, APIs, databases, CI/CD, and all the other wonderfully unglamorous parts of software development.

I know considerably more today than I did when I started.

That's worth something.

## Back to being a developer looking for a job

So that's where I am right now.

I'm applying for frontend and frontend-focused software engineering roles, primarily in Utah or remote. I'm also keeping an eye out for opportunities that could let me broaden my experience into backend development or AI.

I'm going to keep building things, keep learning, and keep applying.

I'll probably spend more time than I would like solving coding problems and trying to remember exactly how Big-O notation works.

I may build some projects specifically because I think they'll make me more marketable.

I may also stare at job descriptions containing the words "rockstar," "ninja," and "fast-paced environment" and wonder what the hell happened to normal job titles.

That's part of the process, I guess.

Getting laid off after nearly five years isn't how I would have chosen to move on from StarkSys. I'm disappointed that it ended this way, and I'll miss the people and the work.

But I'm also proud of what I accomplished there.

Five years ago, I was a much different developer.

Now I get to find out what I can do with everything I've learned.

Time to see what's next.

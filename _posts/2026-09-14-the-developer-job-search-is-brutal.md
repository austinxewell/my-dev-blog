If you're a developer looking for a new job right now, you probably already know how brutal the process can be.

You find a position that looks interesting, start reading through the requirements, and suddenly you're looking at a list of technologies that feels like it was written for an entire development team instead of one person. 😅

Then there are the years of experience requirements.

Then the degree requirements.

Then the application itself.

And somehow, **landing the interview is supposed to be the hard part.**

Honestly?

Sometimes it feels like getting the interview is the _easiest_ part.

## The Never-Ending List of Requirements

Technology moves fast.

Really fast.

Job postings can feel like a checklist of every technology that has become popular in the last decade.

You might see requirements for:

- Multiple frontend frameworks
- Multiple backend languages
- Cloud platforms
- Databases
- Testing frameworks
- CI/CD
- Docker and Kubernetes
- AI and LLM experience
- Several years of experience with technologies that haven't even existed for that long

And then there's usually something along the lines of:

> "Must be a self-starter who can wear many hats."

Sure.

Let me just find a few more hats to put on. 🎩

The frustrating part is that you can be genuinely good at what you do and still feel underqualified when you read these job descriptions.

I've looked at positions and thought:

**"I could absolutely do this job."**

Then I read the requirements and thought:

**"Well... apparently I can't."**

That disconnect can really mess with your confidence.

---

## Experience Is a Weird Requirement

There's something strange about experience requirements in software development.

Companies want years of experience with specific technologies, but the technologies themselves are constantly changing.

You can have years of professional experience building real applications, solving real problems, working with teams, maintaining production code, and dealing with situations that no tutorial prepared you for.

But if you don't have the exact number of years listed next to a particular technology, suddenly it can feel like none of that experience counts.

It does.

Experience isn't just knowing the syntax of a framework.

It's knowing how to investigate a problem.

It's understanding why something was built a certain way.

It's knowing when _not_ to add another abstraction.

It's being able to walk into an unfamiliar codebase and figure out what is happening.

It's learning new technologies because you already understand the fundamentals behind them.

Those skills transfer.

---

## And Then There's the Degree Requirement

For developers who don't have a traditional four-year computer science degree, the job search can add another frustrating layer.

You can have years of professional experience and still run into:

**"Bachelor's degree required."**

And sometimes that's a hard stop.

It doesn't matter how much production code you've written.

It doesn't matter how many problems you've solved.

It doesn't matter how much you've learned outside of a classroom.

You might not even get the opportunity to demonstrate what you're capable of because you didn't check the right box on the application.

That's a tough pill to swallow.

You can know you're capable of doing the work and still never get the opportunity to prove it.

---

## Getting the Interview Is Only the Beginning

Eventually, hopefully, you make it through the application process.

You get the email.

**"We'd like to schedule an interview."**

Awesome. 🎉

Then you remember there's a technical interview.

Now you have to demonstrate that you know what you're doing while someone watches you do it.

Maybe it's algorithms.

Maybe it's data structures.

Maybe it's system design.

Maybe it's a live coding exercise.

Maybe it's debugging unfamiliar code.

Maybe it's some combination of all of them.

And this is where things can get really uncomfortable.

You might have spent years successfully building applications, and suddenly you're staring at a coding problem thinking:

**"Have I actually been a developer this whole time?"**

Yes.

You have.

Your ability to solve one coding problem under interview pressure is not a perfect measurement of your ability to build software.

---

## Imposter Syndrome Is Real

I think imposter syndrome deserves to be talked about more in development.

It's **extremely normal.**

Especially during interviews.

There are incredibly talented developers with years of experience who still look at job postings and wonder whether they're good enough.

There are developers who have been writing software for years who still feel like they don't know enough.

And there are developers who walk into technical interviews convinced they're about to be exposed as a fraud.

I've felt it.

I think most developers have felt some version of it.

The technology landscape is simply too large for one person to know everything.

There will always be another framework.

Another language.

Another architecture pattern.

Another cloud service.

Another thing you haven't learned yet.

Not knowing something doesn't automatically mean you're bad at development.

It means there's something you haven't learned yet.

That's a very different thing.

---

## The Job Search Can Beat You Down

This is probably the part that bothers me the most.

You can send application after application and hear absolutely nothing.

You can make it through multiple interviews and get rejected.

You can spend hours preparing for a technical interview only to walk away feeling like you completely bombed it.

You can see a job posting and immediately decide you're not qualified because you don't check every single box.

After enough of that, it's really easy to start questioning yourself.

**Maybe I'm not as good as I thought I was.**

**Maybe I'm not ready for this level.**

**Maybe I don't belong here.**

Those thoughts can get loud.

But rejection isn't always a measurement of your ability.

Sometimes another candidate had a specific experience the company needed.

Sometimes the company changed direction.

Sometimes there were internal candidates.

Sometimes the hiring process was just weird.

And sometimes, yeah, maybe you weren't ready yet.

That's okay too.

---

## Keep Moving Forward 🚀

The answer isn't to pretend the job search isn't difficult.

It is.

The answer is to keep going anyway.

Keep learning.

Keep building.

Keep studying.

Keep writing.

Keep working on the things you know you need to improve.

Keep putting yourself in situations where you have to grow.

Every interview doesn't have to result in a job to be valuable.

Every rejection doesn't mean you weren't good enough.

Sometimes an interview shows you exactly what you need to study next.

Sometimes a failed technical question exposes a gap you've been able to ignore in your day-to-day work.

That's actually useful information.

It gives you somewhere to go next.

---

## The Goal Is Progress

I'm trying to remind myself that I'm not competing against some imaginary developer who knows everything.

That developer doesn't exist.

I'm competing against the version of myself that existed yesterday.

If I understand something today that I didn't understand last month, that's progress.

If I can explain a concept better than I could six months ago, that's progress.

If I walk into my next technical interview more prepared than the last one, that's progress.

The goal isn't to become the developer who knows everything.

The goal is to become the developer who can keep learning, solve problems, communicate clearly, adapt when things change, and keep moving forward.

The job search might be brutal.

The requirements might be ridiculous sometimes.

The interviews might be uncomfortable.

And imposter syndrome might show up in the passenger seat.

That's okay.

**Just don't hand it the keys. 🚗💨**
